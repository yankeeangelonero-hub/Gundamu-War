# Kitbash Mecha — Experience Wishlist

This is the record of what the experience is meant to be, written before any
technical spec. It is the long-lived statement of intent. Later specs and slices
may defer or constrain parts of it, but they do not edit this file; the deferred
parts stay here as evidence of what was originally wanted. If the thinking shifts,
this file gets a deliberate dated revision rather than a quiet edit.

Working title is Kitbash Mecha. Licensed Gundam names, factions, and lore stay out
of all product, code, and document copy, so anything that sounds like a trademarked
term in this file is a placeholder waiting for a generic rename.

## What this is

Kitbash Mecha is a browser autobattler, but the feeling it is reaching for is not
"win the fight." It is "send someone you care about out into a war in a machine you
built with your own hands, and then watch, unable to touch the controls, hoping
both of them come home."

You are not the pilot. You are the partner engineer. Your craft is the machine —
you kitbash a humanoid mech from a tree of snap-together parts, and you fit it out
for a specific person. That person is a persistent pilot you get attached to over
time. When you send the mech out, you are sending your friend and your work into the
field together. The autobattle that follows is the existing combat layer of the
project — the kitbash build resolved by deterministic simulation and rendered as the
exact machine you assembled — but now it is watched with stakes, because the outcome
lands on a pilot you have a relationship with and a build you are responsible for.

Around that personal loop is a living war. The theatre is a pool of mechs fighting
more or less continuously, and the player is meant to feel like one engineer
contributing to a conflict much larger than their single machine. Near term the war
is a backdrop that drifts; far term it is the headline feature, a faction war that
many players move together. Both are described below, separated by horizon so nobody
mistakes the destination for the next slice.

## The player is the engineer, not the pilot

The whole emotional contract depends on this separation. The player's hands are on
the wrench, never on the trigger. They decide what the machine is, who flies it, and
where it goes; they do not decide what happens once it is out there. That helplessness
during the fight is the point — it is what turns a stat readout into a held breath.

For the near term there is a single pilot. One deep relationship reads stronger than
several shallow ones, and it keeps the first build small. A stable of several pilots
is wanted eventually, but it is explicitly deferred.

## The pilot you build for

The pilot is a persistent character, not a loadout card. They carry across battles,
they gain experience and levels, and they can be hurt. There is no permanent death
in scope — the worst near-term outcome is injury or fatigue that benches them for a
battle while they recover. The intent is stakes without losing the person you got
attached to.

The relationship is between the pilot and the player specifically. The honest way to
say what it is reaching for is an anime-companion hook — a character charming and
present enough that the player gets invested in them as a person, not as a unit. For
now that bond is earned only through what happens in battle: surviving together and
performing well deepens it, and a disaster in a machine that failed them frays it.
The bond is not a separate activity yet. There are no between-mission conversations,
gifts, or courtship mechanics — those are wanted someday and are recorded in the long
horizon, but they are deliberately out of the near-term experience. What the bond
does do is feed back into the fight: a higher bond improves performance and shifts
health and injury outcomes, so investing in the pilot, however indirectly, has a
mechanical payoff and not only a sentimental one.

In combat the pilot matters two ways at once. Some of what they bring folds into the
build before the fight as passive modifiers — the machine simply performs differently
because of who is flying it. And some of it is active: the pilot acts during the duel,
firing abilities as events in their own right, not just sitting as a stat block. That
makes the pilot a genuine participant in the simulation rather than a pre-fight
bonus, which is more expressive and also means every pilot ability has to behave
predictably enough that the same build and the same seed still produce the same fight.
Holding that determinism is a known cost of making pilots active, and it is accepted.

## Skills and parts that need each other

Pilots level up and unlock skills they can use in battle. The player chooses which
skills to equip and can buy into them, so a pilot's kit is something you shape rather
than something handed to you.

The piece that makes this feel like engineering rather than menu shopping is that
skills and parts gate each other. A skill can require a specific part to be installed
before it will function, and a part can require the pilot to have unlocked a matching
ability before it does anything. The example the user reached for is remote drone
weapons that only work once the pilot has unlocked a rare attunement ability — the
weapon is inert without the pilot, and the ability has nothing to act through without
the weapon. (Those particular terms are licensed-genre words and will be renamed; the
mechanic is what matters.) This mutual gating is the core of the fit-out puzzle: the
player is matching a person to a machine so that the two combine into something
neither could be alone.

## Sending them out

Deployment is the emotional hinge of the loop, the moment the player hands the
machine and the pilot over to the war and steps back. Up to that point everything is
in the player's control. After it, nothing is.

The player sends the pilot out into the theatre against enemy mechs. There are no
grunts in the near-term field — the first version is mech against mech, so each fight
reads clearly. Mass-produced grunts that give the war texture are wanted later and
are recorded in the horizon, but they are not in the first cut.

## Watching the fight

The fight itself is the project's existing combat layer: the build the player
assembled, resolved by the deterministic simulator, rendered as the actual mounted
parts moving. What the wishlist adds is the frame around it. The player watches two
things at once — the performance of their own machine against the enemy, and the
larger war environment shifting around that single duel. The personal fight is the
hero moment, but it is happening inside something bigger, and the player should be
able to feel both at the same time: my friend is fighting for their life, and this is
one fight among many on a front that is moving.

## The war that keeps going

The theatre is meant to be a living thing — a pool of mechs fighting more or less
constantly, so the war feels like it exists whether or not the player's mech is in
it. Conceptually the pool holds two kinds of combatant: aces, who are named,
persistent, pilot-driven machines like the player's own and, eventually, other
players' deployed mechs; and grunts, the mass that makes an ace feel rare. The
results of all this fighting are meant to shape the conditions of the war.

Near term this cannot be the full thing, and that is fine. Other players' mechs imply
networking, which the project does not have and is not building yet; in the near term
those would be seeded ghost builds — saved builds replayed deterministically — rather
than live opponents. The near-term war state is a local, single-player echo of the
real thing: a war that drifts from the player's results and an ambient pool of
seeded battles, mechanical enough to function as a grinder that the player experience
can be developed and tested against. It is understood that the specifics here will
change under playtest; the wish is the feeling of a living, drifting war, not any
particular meter. The honest near-term role of the macro layer is a drifting backdrop
that shapes the player's options — which front to deploy to, what enemies are out
there, what salvage turns up — rather than a war the player is trying to win outright.
Winning or losing the war is a horizon-three idea, not a near-term one.

## Homecoming

When the mech comes back, the outcome lands. A win brings the pilot experience and a
level toward new skills, and it brings parts scavenged from the enemies that were
beaten — salvage the player can fit onto their own machine, which closes the loop
back into the workshop. A loss brings less of all that, and it brings consequences for
the pilot: injury, fatigue, a frayed bond. Either way the player returns to the
workshop with a pilot who has changed and a pile of new material to work with, and the
loop begins again.

Salvaged parts can also become research material rather than being fitted directly.
Research is out of scope for now but is noted as an interesting direction — a second
thing to do with salvage besides bolting it on. It stays here as a recorded wish.

## The long horizon

The destination, well beyond the prototype, is a live faction war in the spirit of
Helldivers 2. Players choose one of two factions and fight against each other; the
aggregate of everyone's wins and losses moves an overall war state; and that state is
steered by a game master — the developer — who spawns narrative events on top of it,
like super-weapon offensives or the reveal of secret mech technology. This is a
networked, live-service, operator-tooled layer. It needs a backend, live operations,
and a control surface for the game master, none of which the prototype has. It is
recorded here as the north star so that everything nearer term points toward it, and
it is fenced off clearly as far future so it does not leak into the next slice.

The bond between these layers is the pilot. The persistent, leveling, injurable pilot
that the near-term experience builds is the same ace that the eventual faction war
deploys and risks. The near-term local war is a single-player rehearsal of the live
one. Building the personal loop well is what makes the eventual war worth caring
about, because by the time the war is real the player already has a friend to lose in
it.

The deferred wishes, gathered so they are not forgotten: a stable of multiple pilots
rather than one; between-mission slice-of-life and relationship-building activities
beyond battle outcomes; grunts populating the field alongside aces; research as a
second use for salvage; networked other-player ghosts and then live opponents; and the
full two-faction, GM-steered live war.

## Technology and constraints the user is certain about

These are stated as wishes because they are settled anchors, not derived choices. The
prototype is a browser experience built in plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, runnable
without a build step or a backend. The combat simulation is deterministic — the same
build and the same seed produce the same fight every time — and the simulation is kept
architecturally separate from the animation that plays it back, so a fight can be
skipped or replayed without re-simulating. Part definitions are data, not code, so new
parts are a data change. There is no 3D. And no licensed Gundam IP appears anywhere in
the product, the code, or these documents.

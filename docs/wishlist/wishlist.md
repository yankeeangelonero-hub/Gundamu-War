# Kitbash Mecha — Experience Wishlist

This is the record of what the experience is meant to be, written before any technical
spec. It is the long-lived statement of intent. Later specs and slices may defer or
constrain parts of it, but they do not edit this file under technical pressure; the
deferred parts stay here as evidence of what was originally wanted. When the owner's
thinking shifts, this file gets a deliberate dated revision and the previous version is
kept readable.

Revision 2026-06-06 (r2). This replaces the first draft (kept at
wishlist-r1-2026-06-06-archived.md) after the v0.4 mechanics handoff and the design
discussion that followed. The substantive changes from r1: the loop is now a positive
power fantasy of building toward an unstoppable ace rather than a punishment loop;
the pilot's in-fight meter is framed as sync that builds toward a breakthrough rather
than stress that builds toward a breakdown; the build is governed by a three-layer
constraint model; and the war becomes the defining endgame — a living conflict where the
player's ace fights other real players' builds. The first draft led with the pilot↔player
bond as the near-term hook; r2 keeps that bond as the fantasy but makes the pilot–machine
fit engine the first thing to build, with the relationship layer deferred. Working title
remains Kitbash Mecha; no licensed Gundam IP appears anywhere, so anything that reads like
a trademarked term is a placeholder awaiting a generic rename.

## What this is

Kitbash Mecha is a browser autobattler, but the feeling it reaches for is not "win the
fight." It is "build a machine for someone, send them into a living war, and watch your
creation and your pilot become something feared." The player is the partner engineer, not
the pilot. The craft is the machine — you kitbash a humanoid mech from a tree of
snap-together parts and fit it out for a specific persistent pilot you grow attached to.
The fantasy is constructive and optimistic: I made her unstoppable, and the front knows
her name.

The thing that gives that fantasy depth, and keeps it from being a stat-stacking
spreadsheet, is a single idea carried over from the mechanics handoff and then turned
positive: power is never free, but it is not a tax you suffer — it is potential the pilot
grows into. Every demanding part you bolt on is something she must be able to wield, and
the joy is growing the two of them together until the build that would have overwhelmed her
becomes the build that wins. Identity is the surface, constraint is the engine, and the two
are fused.

## The player is the engineer, not the pilot

The whole emotional contract depends on this separation. The player decides what the
machine is, who flies it, how she is tuned, and where she fights; the player does not touch
the controls once she is deployed. That distance during the fight is the point — it turns a
build readout into something you actually care about the outcome of. For the near term
there is a single pilot; one deep relationship reads stronger than several shallow ones,
and it keeps the first build small. A stable of several pilots is wanted later and is
recorded below as deferred.

## The three-layer constraint model

A build is a negotiation across three layers, all running through the pilot as the
through-line. The mech is never optimized in the abstract — always for and with a specific
person.

The first layer is machine engineering: the substrate of power budget, heat, armor versus
firepower, and weight. This is the least differentiated part — every mech game has it — so
it stays lean and solid, table stakes, not a place to over-tune. The prototype already
seeds it with weight and balance feeding into tilt and action speed.

The second layer is the pilot–machine fit, and it is the star. Can this pilot, at her
current ability, control this machine? Build it too demanding and an undertrained pilot
fights the mech instead of fighting with it. This is a soft relationship, never a hard
gate: an over-demanding mech still works, it just does not yet perform to its ceiling. The
in-fight expression of this is a sync (or resonance) that builds as the pilot and machine
work well together, climbing toward a breakthrough where the machine performs above its
baseline. Pushing a pilot into a demanding-but-reachable frame is how she grows into it.
The same mech is a different object for a different pilot, which is where attachment,
growth, and replay come from. Legibility is the make-or-break requirement here: when a
build underperforms, the player must be able to read why — "the frame outpaces her, drive
the sync up and she breaks through" — and never feel it as bad luck. This needs a pre-deploy
fit readout and an in-fight sync gauge, and because battles are watched rather than driven,
the cause-and-effect on screen has to be close to narrated.

The third layer is pilot behavior: because battles are async and spectated, how the pilot
decides to fight is the player's combat expression. This is the part that can swallow the
project, so it is gated hard. The first builds ship smart defaults so a player who ignores
it still functions; an opt-in set of a few ordered if/then rules comes later as mastery; a
full visual programming graph is a maybe-never. In the long arc, though, this layer matters
more than the handoff first assumed, because against other real players the pilot's decision
logic is part of what makes a stored ace fight like its owner — see the endgame below.

The synthesis, concretely: you build a fast twin-saber bruiser; engineering is fine; the
fit readout warns the frame outpaces the pilot, so sync will be slow to climb in sustained
melee; you choose to detune for a safe steady fight, or push as-is to chase the breakthrough
that makes her a monster in close, or write a rule to pace her exchanges; you deploy and
watch the sync climb, and either it crests into an overdrive that wins clean, or it lags and
she wins ragged and comes home a little stronger for having stretched. That story emerged
from three layers and a person. That is the game.

## Growth that rewards reaching, not grinding

Growth exists to make the ascent feel earned, and it is built to never become a grind. The
guiding rule is that growth rewards strain and novelty, never repetition: pushing into a
demanding fit and facing unfamiliar opponents pays out, while farming the same easy fight
gives less and less. The first draft framed this as denial — caps and diminishing returns
that withhold rewards. r2 reframes it as aspiration: power is not capped, but the reason to
keep getting stronger is that the challenge keeps escalating, so there is always somewhere
to spend new power. Training raises the floor — safe, deliberate practice of fundamentals
that tops out — while real deployment under strain is what breaks the ceiling. Pushing hard
and succeeding leaves a positive permanent mark: a learned trait, a sync milestone, a newly
unlocked ability that opens build space. The gamble is real because the downside of pushing
is a harder fight you might lose or a breakthrough you miss, not harm done to the person you
care about. The kind of growth shapes the kind of attachment: growth from your build choices
says "I shaped who she became," growth from surviving a stretch says "we did that together,"
and both feed the bond.

Skills and parts still need each other. The pilot unlocks abilities as she grows, and the
player equips and invests in them, but a skill can require a specific installed part to
function and a part can require an unlocked pilot ability before it does anything — the
remote-drone weapon that only works once the pilot has the matching attunement, for example
(those genre-specific words will be renamed). That mutual gating is the core of the fit-out
puzzle: matching a person to a machine so the two combine into something neither could be
alone. It is already prototyped in the v0.4 Workshop as seating ability chips into inert
parts.

## Sending them out, and watching

Deployment is the emotional hinge — the moment the player hands the machine and the pilot to
the war and steps back. The fight is the project's existing combat layer: the build the
player assembled, resolved by the deterministic simulator, rendered as the actual mounted
parts. What the wishlist adds is the frame around it. The player watches two things at once:
the sync climbing and the machine performing against an enemy mech, and the larger war
shifting around that single duel. The personal fight is the hero moment, happening inside
something bigger. There are no grunts in the near-term field — the first version is mech
against mech so each fight reads clearly; mass combatants come later.

## The war is the point

The defining endgame, which everything else builds toward, is a living war where the
player's ace fights other real players' mechs — machines, pilots, and behavior built by
real people — in a persistent, contested front. This is what makes the positive power loop
never deflate: a population of humans building unstoppable aces is an opposition that never
gets solved or trivialized, an escalation engine no designed content ladder can match. The
fantasy is realized socially — your ace is feared because other players fear her — and the
attachment becomes pride and reputation. In this frame, "build an OP ace" means OP relative
to a living meta, which is exactly why the constraint depth matters: the fit puzzle and the
build negotiation are the skill ceiling that separates good players from great ones.

This is async, not live: the player fights other players' stored builds, simulated on their
own machine, and the aggregate of everyone's battles moves the state of the war. The
keystone that makes it fair is determinism — the same build and seed produce the same fight
every time, so a stored ace can be fought reproducibly and a server can re-simulate to
verify a result without trusting the client. The architectural commitment that keeps this
reachable without a rewrite is that opponent builds are an injected data source: the game
simulates any build identically whether it came from a static file, a designer, or a real
player over the network. Near term the war is local — the player fights seeded ghost builds
that are shaped like real-player builds, with no backend — and the only thing that changes
when the backend arrives is where the opponent builds come from. The two-faction,
game-master-steered version of the war, with developer-spawned events, remains the far
horizon on top of this.

Because the endgame is competitive and against other people, the positive valence is close
to required, not just preferred: losing a fight to a real player must never permanently harm
the pilot you are attached to. The positive-loop decision and the PvP-endgame decision
reinforce each other.

## Homecoming

When the mech returns, the outcome lands as growth. A win brings the pilot experience toward
new skills, a higher sync ceiling, and salvage — parts taken from beaten enemies that feed
back into the workshop, or, later, research material. Pushing a stretch fight and surviving
it leaves a breakthrough. A loss brings less of all that and a harder road, but not injury
to the person. Either way the player returns to the workshop with a pilot who has grown and
new material to build with, and the loop begins again, always reaching upward.

## What is deferred, kept here so it is not forgotten

A stable of multiple pilots rather than one. Slice-of-life and relationship-building
activities beyond battle outcomes. Grunts populating the field alongside aces. Research as a
second use for salvage. The opt-in behavior-rule layer, and any visual AI programming beyond
it. The networked backend for real-player opponents, and then the two-faction,
game-master-steered live war with developer events. These are wanted; they are simply not
the near-term build.

## References that anchor the feeling

The closest proven cousins for the positive build-toward-an-ace loop are Monster Hunter
(build, take on a harder hunt, build again — the aspiration treadmill done right) and the
ARPG power curve of Path of Exile or Diablo (compounding power against content that scales
to meet it). The pilot–machine fit draws on Evangelion's sync ratio, kept here as a high to
reach rather than a leash. The attachment-through-growth is the "develop a star" fantasy of
a sports-management game or a JRPG party member who becomes a powerhouse. Darkest Dungeon is
worth studying for one thing only — how it makes a stress-style meter legible — but it is
explicitly not the emotional model, because its loop is about managing loss and this one is
about earning ascent.

## Technology the owner is committing to

These are stated as wishes because they are settled anchors, not derived choices. The product
target is Steam PC first and mobile-app compatible second, with optional web demos/playtests.
The build target is Godot 4.6 using GDScript, chosen after evaluating the engine against the
rigged-2D kitbash, the effects pipeline, deterministic simulation, native desktop/mobile
release paths, and the server-side re-simulation the PvP endgame needs. GDScript remains the
near-term language because it is Godot-native and preserves optional web export; C# is not
part of the v0.4 spike. The reasoning is recorded in
docs/adrs/2026-06-06-build-stack-decision.md and remains provisional pending a confirmation
spike. The simulation is kept a pure, deterministic, renderer-agnostic core, separate from
the animation that plays it back, so a fight can be skipped, replayed, or re-simulated
server-side from the same build and seed. Part definitions are data, not code. There is no
3D. No licensed Gundam IP appears anywhere — specifically no V-fin antenna, no split
twin-eye visor, no RX-78 silhouette or trim; original identity uses a mono-eye, a single
visor band, or a full-face sensor plate. The product target is Steam PC first and mobile-app
compatible second. Web export is optional for demos/playtests rather than the primary product
platform. The prototype runs locally with no backend; the backend that the war endgame needs
is a later addition the architecture must not preclude.
